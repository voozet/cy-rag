section: Incident Response
---
NIST Special Publication 800-61 (Computer Security Incident Handling Guide) presents a lifecycle model for incident response built around four major phases: Preparation; Detection and Analysis; Containment, Eradication, and Recovery; and Post-Incident Activity. In the Preparation phase, an organization establishes an incident response capability, including policies, communication plans, tooling, and training, and also works to prevent incidents by securing networks, systems, and applications so that the number of incidents is reduced. The Detection and Analysis phase covers the identification of precursors and indicators of a possible incident, the triage and validation of alerts to confirm whether an actual incident has occurred, and the prioritization of incidents based on functional impact, information impact, and recoverability effort. Accurate and timely detection is described as one of the most difficult aspects of incident response because indicators can be numerous, subtle, and generated by many different sources such as intrusion detection systems, antivirus software, log analysis, and user reports. The Containment, Eradication, and Recovery phase involves selecting a containment strategy appropriate to the incident (balancing the need to limit damage against the need to preserve evidence and maintain business operations), identifying and eliminating the root cause of the incident such as removing malware or disabling breached accounts, and restoring systems to normal operation while confirming that they are functioning correctly. NIST notes that containment strategies vary by incident type and should be defined in advance, considering factors like potential damage to and theft of resources, need for evidence preservation, service availability, time and resources required, effectiveness of the strategy, and duration of the solution. The final phase, Post-Incident Activity, emphasizes learning and improving: conducting "lessons learned" meetings with all involved parties, documenting what happened and how it was handled, and using this information to update policies, procedures, and technical controls to prevent recurrence and improve the response to future incidents. The guide also stresses that incident response is not strictly linear; organizations often cycle between Detection and Analysis and Containment/Eradication/Recovery as new information emerges during handling of a single incident.
